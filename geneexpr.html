Regulation of Gene Expression in Eukaryotes

How cells decide which genes to express — from chromatin packing to protein degradation — and why it matters for medicine.

Chromatin Transcription Factors mRNA Processing Alternative Splicing Ubiquitin–Proteasome Hormones & HREs
01
Overview: Levels of Regulation
The five control points
▾

Eukaryotes regulate gene expression at multiple levels — this gives far finer control than prokaryotes, and allows the same genome to produce hundreds of different cell types.

By Regulating…
  • Chromatin structure – DNA accessibility
  • Transcription initiation – whether RNA Pol II binds
  • Translation initiation – ribosome access to mRNA
By Modifying…
  • mRNA – splicing, capping, editing, poly-A
  • Proteins – post-translational modification, degradation
🔑 Core Principle
Gene expression can be turned on or off at any step between DNA and functional protein. Each level is an independent control point.
02
Chromatin Structure
Nucleosomes, histones, eu- vs heterochromatin
▾

What is chromatin?

In eukaryotes, DNA is wrapped around proteins called histones to form a compact protein-DNA complex called chromatin.

The Nucleosome

The basic repeating unit is the nucleosome: ~147 bp of DNA wound around an octamer of histone proteins (H2A, H2B, H3, H4 — two copies each). Histone H1 sits at the entry/exit point and links adjacent nucleosomes.

Euchromatin (Open)
  • Loosely packed histones
  • DNA accessible to transcription machinery
  • Actively transcribed genes
  • Histone acetyltransferases (HATs) loosen histone-DNA bonds by neutralising histone positive charges
Heterochromatin (Closed)
  • Tightly packed histones
  • DNA inaccessible — genes generally not transcribed
  • Inactive / silent regions
  • Histone deacetylases (HDACs) restore positive charges → strong DNA-histone bonding → coiling

Histone Acetylation (the key switch)

Lysine residues on histone tails carry a positive charge and bind tightly to negatively-charged DNA. When HATs add an acetyl group (from Acetyl-CoA) to lysine, the positive charge is neutralised — DNA-histone interaction weakens, chromatin opens, and transcription can proceed. HDACs reverse this.

Clinical link
HDAC inhibitors (e.g. vorinostat) are used in cancer therapy — they force tumour-suppressor genes back into euchromatin so they can be re-expressed.
03
Transcription Initiation
Transcription factors, enhancers, repressors
▾

Transcription Factors (TFs)

RNA polymerase II (which transcribes protein-coding genes) cannot bind the promoter on its own. It needs transcription factors — proteins that assemble at the promoter and recruit RNA Pol II.

  • TFs present → RNA Pol II binds → gene is expressed
  • TFs absent → RNA Pol II cannot bind (or binds only rarely) → gene not expressed

Enhancers and Activators

Activator proteins bind to DNA segments upstream from the promoter called enhancers. The enhancer-activator complex then loops to contact the transcription initiation complex at the TATA box (core promoter) via coactivator proteins, dramatically boosting transcription.

Silencers and Repressors

Conversely, repressor proteins bind to DNA sequences called silencers. They can block activator binding or recruit HDACs to condense chromatin and shut down transcription.

Activator binds enhancer → Loop contacts TATA box → Coactivators / basal TFs assemble → RNA Pol II recruits → Transcription begins
Remember
Enhancers can act even when thousands of base pairs away from the promoter — they work by DNA looping, not proximity.
04
Regulation by Modifying mRNA
Pre-mRNA processing, splicing, RNA editing
▾

Pre-mRNA → mature mRNA

After transcription, the initial transcript (pre-mRNA / hnRNA) undergoes several modifications before leaving the nucleus:

  1. 5' Capping – a 7-methylguanosine cap is added to the 5' end. Protects mRNA from degradation and is required for ribosome recognition.
  2. Intron removal (splicing) – non-coding introns are excised by the spliceosome; exons are joined together.
  3. 3' Polyadenylation – a poly-A tail of ~200 adenine residues is added to the 3' end. Protects mRNA and aids export and translation.
  4. Alternative splicing – different combinations of exons can be included, producing different proteins from one gene.

Alternative Splicing

The same gene can generate multiple protein isoforms. E.g., exons 1+2+3+4+5 → Protein A; exons 1+2+4+5 → Protein B (different function). This massively expands the proteome beyond the ~20,000 human genes.

Translation Initiation Control

Regulatory proteins can bind to the 5' untranslated region (UTR) of mRNAs and prevent ribosome attachment, blocking translation. Additionally, mRNAs lacking a poly-A tail cannot be translated. Example: some egg cytoplasm mRNAs are stored in a translationally silent state — after fertilisation, a poly-A tail is added, switching translation on.

RNA Editing

Even after splicing, individual bases in the mRNA can be chemically altered:

Classic Example – Apolipoprotein B (ApoB)
ApoB mRNA is produced in both the liver and small intestine. In the intestine only, a cytidine deaminase converts the C in codon CAA (glutamine) to U, creating UAA — a stop codon. This produces a shorter ApoB-48 protein in the intestine vs full-length ApoB-100 in the liver. Two different proteins from one gene!
05
mRNA Stability: The Transferrin Receptor
Iron-responsive elements (IREs) & IRPs
▾

Context

Transferrin is a plasma protein that transports iron. It binds to transferrin receptors (TfRs) on cell surfaces. The cell must regulate TfR expression based on its iron needs.

The IRE–IRP system

  • The TfR mRNA has iron-responsive elements (IREs) — stem-loop structures — in its 3' UTR.
  • IREs can bind iron-regulatory proteins (IRPs).
Low intracellular iron
  • IRPs have no iron to bind
  • IRPs bind to IREs on TfR mRNA
  • mRNA is stabilised → more TfR synthesised
  • Cell imports more iron via transferrin
High intracellular iron
  • IRPs preferentially bind iron (not IREs)
  • IREs are unbound → mRNA is degraded
  • Less TfR made
  • Iron import decreases
Elegant feedback
The IRE–IRP system is a classic post-transcriptional feedback loop: iron regulates the stability of the very mRNA that encodes the protein responsible for importing iron.
06
Regulation by Modifying Proteins
Ubiquitin–proteasome system & lysosomal degradation
▾

The Ubiquitin–Proteasome Pathway

Cells need to degrade damaged, misfolded, or regulatory proteins in a controlled, ATP-dependent way.

  1. Ubiquitination: Small globular ubiquitin proteins are covalently attached to the target protein.
  2. Polyubiquitin chain: Multiple ubiquitin molecules are added, forming a chain — this is the degradation signal.
  3. Recognition by proteasome: The 26S proteasome recognises the polyubiquitin tag.
  4. Unfolding & degradation: The proteasome unfolds the protein and cleaves it into short peptides (amino acids enter the amino acid pool).
  5. Ubiquitin recycling: Ubiquitin molecules are released and reused.
⚡ Energy note
The ubiquitin–proteasome pathway is ATP-dependent. This distinguishes it from lysosomal degradation, which uses acid hydrolysis and does not require energy (ATP).

Lysosomal Degradation

Proteins can also be degraded in lysosomes via acid hydrolases. This is an energy-independent process and is particularly important for extracellular proteins taken up by endocytosis.

💊
Clinical Pearl – Proteasome Inhibitors Bortezomib (Velcade) inhibits the 26S proteasome and is used to treat multiple myeloma. By blocking protein degradation, it causes accumulation of pro-apoptotic proteins in cancer cells.
07
Hormones & Gene Programmes
HREs, cis/trans elements, intracellular vs cell-surface receptors
▾

How hormones regulate genes

Hormones act as signals to switch entire gene programmes on or off. They do so via hormone-responsive elements (HREs) — specific DNA sequences that act as cis-acting regulatory sequences. These are bound by trans-acting protein factors (transcription factors).

Intracellular Receptors (e.g. Steroids)
  • Steroid hormones (e.g. cortisol) are lipid-soluble → cross the membrane
  • Bind to intracellular receptor → conformational change exposes DNA-binding domain
  • Hormone-receptor complex (dimer) enters nucleus
  • Binds to GRE (glucocorticoid response element)
  • Recruits coactivators → activates RNA Pol II → gene transcription
Cell-Surface Receptors (e.g. Glucagon)
  • Peptide hormones (e.g. glucagon) are water-soluble → cannot cross the membrane
  • Bind cell-surface receptor → activates adenylyl cyclase → ATP → cAMP
  • cAMP activates PKA → phosphorylates CREB transcription factor
  • Phospho-CREB binds to CRE (cAMP response element)
  • Activates transcription machinery
Terminology
Cis-acting elements (HREs, enhancers, silencers) are DNA sequences on the same chromosome as the gene they regulate. Trans-acting factors (TFs, activators, repressors) are proteins encoded elsewhere that diffuse to and bind the cis elements.

High-Yield Exam Facts
▾
  • Nucleosome core = H2A, H2B, H3, H4 (×2). H1 is the linker histone.
  • Euchromatin = acetylated (open, active). Heterochromatin = deacetylated (closed, silent).
  • RNA Pol II needs transcription factors; alone it cannot initiate transcription.
  • Enhancers work by DNA looping and can be far from the promoter.
  • ApoB: liver makes ApoB-100; intestine makes ApoB-48 via C→U RNA editing (CAA→UAA stop codon).
  • Low iron → IRPs bind IREs → TfR mRNA stabilised → more iron uptake.
  • High iron → IRPs bind iron → IREs unbound → TfR mRNA degraded → less uptake.
  • Ubiquitin-proteasome: ATP-dependent. Lysosomal degradation: energy-independent.
  • Steroids use intracellular receptors (GRE). Glucagon uses cell-surface receptor → cAMP → CREB/CRE.
