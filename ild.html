What is ILD?

Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD) = a multitude of diseases causing FIBROSIS of the pulmonary parenchyma/interstitium. The hallmark is fibrosis or scarring, resulting in a restrictive ventilatory defect.

  • Normal lung: spongy, full of air spaces
  • Fibrotic lung: hard, dense, poor function — alveolar walls thickened, gas diffusion impaired → hypoxaemia and dyspnoea
5 Main Aetiological Categories

Occupational Environmental

  • Inorganic: Asbestosis, silicosis, coal, beryllium
  • Organic: EAA/hypersensitivity pneumonitis (mouldy hay, bird faeces, cotton)

Idiopathic

  • IPF (Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis)
  • Sarcoidosis
  • Langerhans cell histiocytosis

Drug / Treatment

  • Amiodarone, bleomycin, radiotherapy

Connective Tissue Disorders

  • SLE, Rheumatoid arthritis, scleroderma, dermatomyositis

Post-infectious

  • TB

Diagnosis
Clinicopathological Correlation (CPC) is essential. ILD is diagnosed by combining radiographic features with clinical signs and symptoms. The interstitium is not normally visible radiographically — it only becomes visible when diseased (increased volume and attenuation).
Pathogenesis (General)

Particle Inhalation → Pneumoconiosis

Pneumoconiosis = lung disease caused by inhalation of dusts/particles. Pathogenicity depends on:

  • Size: 1–5 µm most dangerous (reach alveoli); >5–10 µm rarely reach distal alveoli; <0.5 µm act like gases and do little damage
  • Shape: Long, thin fibres (e.g. asbestos) more pathogenic
  • Also: chemical composition, concentration, solubility, particle reactivity, duration of exposure
Pathogenesis of Pneumoconiosis Particles inhaled → taken up by alveolar macrophages → macrophages release fibrogenic factors (TNF, PDGF, IGF-1, fibronectin → recruit fibroblasts, collagen production), toxic factors (proteases, O₂ free radicals → lung injury), and pro-inflammatory factors (LTB4, IL-8, IL-6, TNF, MIP-1α → recruit inflammatory cells) → ultimately FIBROSIS.
Coal Coal Workers' Pneumoconiosis

Coal components

Carbon, trace metals, inorganic minerals, crystalline silica

TypeFeaturesComplications
Pulmonary Anthracosis Most innocuous. Carbon pigments taken up by macrophages, accumulate along lymphatics and in lymph nodes. Seen in urban dwellers & smokers. None usually
Simple CWP Coal macules (dust-laden macrophages) + coal nodules (collagen fibres). Focal dust around respiratory bronchioles. Upper zone predominant. Centrilobular emphysema. Usually NOT functionally disabling.
Complicated CWP / PMF Progressive Massive Fibrosis — develops after 10–20 years. Coalescence of nodules → intensely black scars. Dense collagen + pigment, often central necrosis (ischaemia). Upper zone. Pulmonary dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale
Caplan Syndrome Co-existence of rheumatoid arthritis + pneumoconiosis → distinctive nodular pulmonary lesions developing rapidly. Central necrosis surrounded by palisading fibroblasts, plasma cells, macrophages with coal dust + collagen. Also occurs in asbestosis & silicosis
Silica Silicosis

Cause

Inhalation of crystalline silica. Crystalline forms (quartz, cristobalite, tridymite) more toxic/fibrogenic than amorphous forms.

Occupations: Rock cutting, mining, drilling, tunnelling, stonemasons, sandblasters, glass/pottery making

FormFeatures
Acute silicosis Very high exposure, rapid onset. Tachypnoea, cough, cyanosis, respiratory failure. Histology: interstitial inflammation + proteinaceous fluid in alveolar spaces.
Chronic (nodular) Prolonged exposure. Characteristic fibrotic silicotic nodules. Upper zone and subpleural predominant.
Silicotic nodule pathway: Silica inhaled → macrophages take up particles → macrophage disintegrates, releases silica → fibroblasts activated → fibrous tissue develops → dense nodule of scar tissue (whorled collagen).
Complicated (conglomerate) PMF — expansion and coalescence of nodules, destruction of lung parenchyma.
Silicosis associations: Increased risk of TB · Caplan syndrome · Lung carcinoma · Progressive respiratory failure, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale
Asbestos Asbestosis

Background

Asbestos = family of crystalline hydrated silicates. Sources: construction materials, insulation, cement, roofing, electric motors, textiles.

Fibre types:

  • Serpentine (curly, flexible) — e.g. chrysotile (most used in industry)
  • Amphibole (straight, stiff, brittle) — e.g. amosite, crocidolite — more pathogenic

Pathology of Asbestosis

  • Fibres inhaled → surrounded by macrophages → coated in iron → dumbbell-shaped ferruginous bodies
  • Latency period: 10–30 years
  • Diffuse pulmonary interstitial fibrosis — begins around respiratory bronchioles/alveolar ducts, extends to alveolar sacs/alveoli
  • Enlarged air spaces enclosed by thick fibrous walls → honeycombing
  • Begins in lower lobes subpleurally, progresses upward

Organic Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis (EAA)
Key distinction Inorganic dusts cause direct tissue injury. Organic antigens cause a Type III hypersensitivity reaction — circulating antibodies react with inhaled antigen, precipitating antigen/antibody complexes in the lung. Can progress to chronic Type IV hypersensitivity → pulmonary fibrosis in upper zones.

Clinical Features (Acute)

  • Acute febrile episodes
  • Headache, malaise, cough, dyspnoea
  • Bibasal crepitations
  • Onset 6–8 hours after antigen exposure
  • Acute symptoms last ~24 hours

Common Antigens

  • Farmer's lung: Mouldy hay (thermophilic actinomyces, Aspergillus)
  • Pigeon fancier's lung: Avian antigens (bird faeces, feathers)
  • Cotton fibre (byssinosis)

Pathology

Alveolar walls infiltrated by lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages, granulomas. Chronic phase → fibrosis (often subtle clinically).

Investigations & Management

  • History essential — exposure history, timing, repeat episodes
  • Serum precipitins, specific IgE
  • Management: Avoidance of allergen, air-driven mask, steroids
  • Good prognosis overall if allergen avoided
Sarcoidosis

Definition

Multisystemic disease of unknown aetiology characterised by non-caseating granulomata in many tissues. Involves lungs in 90–95% of cases.

Sarcoidosis is a diagnosis of exclusion — many diseases (TB, berylliosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, silicosis, lymphoma) also cause granulomatous inflammation. Always requires clinicopathological correlation.

Epidemiology

  • 11/100,000 in whites; 36/100,000 in African Americans
  • Female > Male (2:1)
  • Age of onset: 25–40 years
  • High incidence: Sweden, Denmark, US African Americans
  • Higher prevalence in non-smokers

Aetiology

  • Unknown — disordered immune regulation in genetically predisposed individuals
  • T cells central; accumulation of CD4 T lymphocytes + IL-2 release
  • Possible environmental antigen trigger
  • No unequivocal infectious cause

Clinical Features

Presentations

  • Often asymptomatic / incidental (e.g. screening CXR)
  • 30% present with respiratory symptoms: SOB, dry cough
  • Non-specific: fever, fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, anorexia
  • Peripheral lymphadenopathy, cutaneous lesions, eye involvement, neurosarcoid

Named Syndromes

  • Löfgren's syndrome: Fever + erythema nodosum + polyarthritis (often ankles) + bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy
  • Mikulicz's syndrome: Combined uveoparotid involvement (uveitis + parotitis)
CXR findings in sarcoidosis: Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy · Right paratracheal strip enlargement · Bilateral infiltrates predominantly in the mid zones (upper zone on zone table)
Investigations & Diagnosis
  • CXR, CT thorax
  • PFTs (restrictive defect)
  • ECG (cardiac involvement)
  • Bloods: Calcium often raised (granulomas produce 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D), alkaline phosphatase, serum ACE (elevated in ~75%), renal/liver/bone profile
  • Tissue sampling: most common site = hilar nodes via EBUS-TBNA (endobronchial ultrasound, transbronchial needle aspiration) → shows non-caseating granulomatous inflammation
  • Sample MUST go to microbiology (C&S) to exclude TB and other infectious causes
  • Case discussed at multidisciplinary meeting
Clinical Course

Course

  • Unpredictable; may remain asymptomatic
  • Periods of activity interspersed with remission
  • Respiratory involvement progresses to diffuse interstitial fibrosis in 10–15% → pulmonary hypertension → cor pulmonale
  • Visual impairment possible
  • Usually steroid responsive

Pathology

  • Non-caseating granulomas: central collection of epithelioid macrophages + giant cells, surrounded by lymphocytes
  • No central necrosis (contrast with TB!)
  • Can progress to fibrosis with hyalinisation of granulomas
IPF Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis

Definition

Pulmonary disorder of unknown aetiology characterised histologically by diffuse interstitial fibrosis. Diagnosis of exclusion — must rule out asbestosis, CTDs, drugs etc. before calling it "idiopathic".

Demographics: M > F; 2/3 >60 years

Histological term: Usual Interstitial Pneumonia (UIP)

Pathogenesis

Unknown injury / antigen
→
Alveolar wall injury
→
Alveolitis (oedema + inflammatory cells)
→
Persistent injury → macrophage/lymphocyte/neutrophil activation
→
Fibroblast proliferation + collagen production
→
Progressive interstitial fibrosis

Key mediators: PDGF, TGF-β, TNF-α, MMPs. Macrophages activated by immune complexes + cytokines from T cells → release oxidants/proteases → injure Type I pneumocytes → Type II pneumocyte hyperplasia (repair) → fibroblast activation → ECM deposition.

Pathologic Features (UIP)

  • Heterogeneous appearance: areas of fibrosis/inflammation alternating with areas of normal lung (temporal heterogeneity)
  • Inflammation usually patchy
  • Advanced: "honeycomb change" — lung spaces separated by inflammatory fibrous tissue
  • Fibrosis begins subpleurally in lower lobes

Clinical Features

  • Insidious onset: dry cough + progressive dyspnoea
  • Age >60 years
  • Clubbing
  • Physical exam: characteristic "velcro crackles" on inspiration (bibasal)
  • Progressive disease → cyanosis, cor pulmonale (loud P2, parasternal heave, raised JVP), peripheral oedema

Investigations

  • CXR: bilateral interstitial shadowing, patchy/nodular, lower zone bilateral
  • HRCT Thorax: honeycombing + traction bronchiectasis, reticular opacities, subpleural lower zone predominant
  • PFTs: restrictive defect (↓ FVC, ↓ TLC, ↓ DLCO)
  • Bloods: FBC, U&E, LFTs, calcium, ABG
  • +/- Biopsy via VATS (video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery) — only if atypical presentation
IPF vs Sarcoidosis — key differences: IPF: M>F, >60 yrs, lower zone, no granulomas, poor prognosis. Sarcoidosis: F>M, 25–40 yrs, upper/mid zone, non-caseating granulomas, often steroid responsive.
ARDS Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome

Definition

Syndrome of reduced pulmonary gas exchange caused by diffuse injury to the alveolar-capillary barrier. A form of noncardiogenic pulmonary oedema resulting from acute alveolar damage.

Direct Lung Injury

  • Pneumonia
  • Aspiration of gastric contents

Indirect Lung Injury

  • Sepsis
  • Severe trauma with shock
  • Multiple transfusions
Pathophysiology

Alveolar Epithelial Barrier

  • Type I pneumocytes: Flat, 90% of alveolar surface, easily injured
  • Type II pneumocytes: Cuboidal, more resistant to injury, produce surfactant, proliferate to repair
Acute Phase — Diffuse Alveolar Damage (DAD) Injury → diffuse alveolar damage → sloughing of bronchial and alveolar epithelial cells → protein-rich hyaline membranes form on the denuded basement membrane (hallmark histological finding). Neutrophils accumulate in early stages. Alveoli filled with proteinaceous fluid.

Ventilation-Induced Lung Injury (VILI)

Mechanical ventilation can worsen injury. Alveolar overdistension and repeated collapse/reopening stimulate inflammatory cytokine release (volutrauma, atelectrauma).

Outcomes

  • If uncomplicated → rapid resolution
  • Others progress to fibrotic lung injury
  • Most patients will die without supplemental O₂ and assisted ventilation
Clinical Diagnosis
ARDS Diagnostic Criteria (Berlin Definition): In an at-risk patient, rapid onset of respiratory failure within 1 week of clinical insult + bilateral pulmonary infiltrates on CXR (not explained by cardiac failure/fluid overload) + PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤300 mmHg.

Clinical Features

  • Rapid onset respiratory failure
  • Arterial hypoxaemia — difficult to treat
  • Tachypnoea, tachycardia
  • Cyanosis
  • Bilateral crackles

Investigations

  • CXR: bilateral pulmonary infiltrates ("white out")
  • ABG: severe hypoxaemia
  • Echo/BNP to exclude cardiogenic oedema
  • Identify underlying cause
Management principles: Treat underlying cause · Lung-protective ventilation (low tidal volume 6 mL/kg) · PEEP to keep alveoli open · Prone positioning in severe ARDS · Supportive care (fluids, nutrition, sedation)
