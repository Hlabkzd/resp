Respiratory Viruses — Study Guide

RCSI Year 1 Semester 2 · Dr. Ellen Browne · 11 May 2026

What are respiratory tract viruses?

⚠ Case scenario: surgical intern, January, fever 39.3°C + myalgia + lassitude = INFLUENZA, not the common cold. Common cold has no significant fever and is systemically mild.

3 categories of infection

  • Upper RTI (URTI) — common cold, sinusitis, pharyngitis
  • Lower RTI (LRTI) — pneumonia, bronchitis, bronchiolitis
  • Respiratory complications of systemic infections — varicella pneumonitis, measles pneumonitis
Can start as URTI then progress to LRTI

Transmission of respiratory viruses

Direct spread (principal route)

Person-to-person via respiratory droplets from coughing or sneezing.

Large droplets (>100µm) travel ballistically and settle quickly. Small droplets/aerosols (<1µm) can remain airborne for several hours.

Indirect spread — fomite

Person touches a contaminated surface, then touches their mouth, nose, or eye. The contaminated object = a fomite.

Infection control — how to prevent spread

  • Immunisation where possible (e.g. annual influenza vaccine)
  • Standard precautions for ALL patients
  • PLUS droplet (+/- airborne) precautions
  • PLUS Point of Care Risk Assessment (PCRA)

Influenza viruses

⚠ Influenza = a virus. NOT the common cold. NOT Haemophilus influenzae (that is a bacterium).

Classification — 3 types

  • Type A Infects mammals and birds. Causes seasonal epidemics AND pandemics. Main reservoir = birds (replicates in avian GIT → high faecal shedding → humans → human-to-human).
  • Type B Humans only. Outbreaks every 2–4 years. No pandemics.
  • Type C Humans only. Uncommon, causes milder disease.

Virus structure

  • Enveloped RNA virus
  • Genome = 8 segments
  • 2 surface glycoproteins determine subtype:
  • HA — Haemagglutinin Binds to receptors on host cells (H1, H2, H3…)
  • NA — Neuraminidase Cleaves sialic acid to release virus from cell; prevents clumping. Drug target for oseltamivir and zanamivir.

Antigenic drift → seasonal epidemics

Minor change
  • Gradual accumulation of point mutations in HA and NA genes
  • Caused by errors in viral RNA polymerase during replication
  • Results in slightly new virus each year → yearly epidemics
  • Partial immunity in population; most severe in elderly and infants
  • H3 subtype produces the most severe outbreaks

Antigenic shift → pandemics

Major change
  • Genetic reassortment: 2 different influenza strains co-infect one cell → exchange gene segments → novel virus
  • Novel surface proteins = little or no population immunity
  • High attack rates with excess morbidity and mortality across ALL age groups
  • Leads to pandemic

Pathogenesis — the infection cycle

  • Entry: Droplets land on mucous membranes → enters via respiratory epithelial cells → attaches to columnar epithelium
  • Attachment: HA binds host cell receptors; NA cleaves sialic acid to release new virus particles from cell
  • Immune evasion: Antigenic shift/drift changes surface antigens; virus-induced lymphocyte dysfunction; interferon response contributes to clinical features (fever, myalgia)
  • Cellular damage: Diffuse inflammation of trachea and bronchi → may progress to ulcerative necrotizing tracheobronchitis; loss of cilia → impaired mucociliary clearance → increased risk of bacterial superinfection
  • Spread: Primarily droplet (+/- aerosol)

Clinical features

  • Abrupt onset fever, cough, and myalgia lasting a few days
  • More persistent weakness and depression after acute phase
  • Incubation period: 1–2 days
  • 95% of exposed individuals become symptomatic within 3 days
  • Infectious for up to 5–7 days after symptom onset

Complications

  • Primary influenza pneumonia
  • Secondary bacterial pneumonia — S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, S. aureus
  • Myositis / myocarditis
  • CNS: seizures, encephalopathy, encephalitis
  • Reye's syndrome — encephalopathy + liver failure (now very rare)
  • Death

Treatment

  • Mainly supportive — antipyretics, analgesia
  • If hospitalised: oxygen + critical care review if indicated
  • Antivirals = neuraminidase inhibitors:
  • Oseltamivir (oral) — can also be used as post-exposure prophylaxis in at-risk patients
  • Zanamivir (inhaled)
  • Advise annual influenza vaccination going forward

Vaccination — who should receive it?

Inactivated virus, administered annually in advance of influenza season (September/October).

  • People aged 60 and older
  • Any individual >6 months at increased risk: chronic illness needing regular medical follow-up, immunosuppression, all pregnant women (any gestation) and up to 6 weeks postpartum, morbid obesity, long-term aspirin therapy, nursing home residents, pig/poultry/waterfowl workers
  • All children 2–17 years (live intranasal vaccine)
  • Healthcare workers — ~30% of influenza infections are asymptomatic but still infectious; HCWs can transmit to vulnerable patients and influenza can kill them
  • Family/carers/household contacts of high-risk patients (may be considered)

Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV)

Epidemiology

  • Most common severe LRTI in infants and children under 2 years
  • Commonest cause of hospital admissions in young children
  • Source of infection for infant = typically adults and older children (who have mild or no symptoms)
  • Worldwide distribution with seasonal peaks in temperate climates (winter)
  • Re-infection is common
  • Highly infectious — spread facilitated by crowding, crèche/preschool attendance, siblings attending school

Clinical presentation by age

  • Infants / children <2yr Severe LRTI — bronchiolitis
  • Older children / adults Usually upper respiratory symptoms only
  • Immunocompromised Severe disease at any age

RSV bronchiolitis — clinical signs

  • Acute inflammation of the small airways (bronchioles)
  • Respiratory distress: tachypnoea, nasal flaring, wheeze
  • Apnoea
  • Irritability, cyanosis
  • Prominent intercostal retraction (lower airway obstruction)
  • CXR: flat diaphragms + hyperlucent lower lung fields = hyperinflation

Management

  • Mainly supportive: antipyretics, oxygen
  • Antiviral ribavirin — consider for high-risk infants with severe disease; clear benefit not conclusively demonstrated
  • Isolate with droplet precautions

Prevention — new EU-authorised developments

2 forms of passive immunisation for infants:
  • Nirsevimab — long-acting monoclonal antibody, single IM injection administered directly to infant. National pilot programme underway in Ireland.
  • RSVpreF (Abrysvo, Pfizer) — maternal vaccine; provides infant protection through transplacental antibody transfer. Not yet part of free national programme in Ireland.
2 forms of RSV vaccine for older people (not yet free in Ireland):
  • RSVPreF3 (Arexvy, produced by GSK)
  • RSVpreF (Abrysvo, produced by Pfizer)

Other respiratory viruses

Parainfluenza virus

  • 4 serotypes (Types 1–4, including 4a and 4b)
  • Accounts for 6–9% of respiratory viral infections
  • Incubation period: 3–6 days
  • Classic syndrome: Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis) — harsh brassy cough, worse at night
  • 2nd most common cause of childhood respiratory hospitalisation (after RSV)
  • Can also cause bronchiolitis
  • Moderate-to-severe infection in immunocompromised patients
  • Treatment: supportive only

Adenovirus

  • ~50 different serotypes
  • Causes RTI, diarrhoea, AND conjunctivitis
  • RTIs include: common cold, pharyngitis, bronchitis, bronchiolitis, pneumonia (winter and spring)
  • Pneumonia more common in boys than girls, and in immunocompromised patients
  • Treatment: generally supportive

Diagnosis of respiratory viral infections

Clinical assessment

  • History including vaccination history
  • Physical examination

Respiratory samples used

  • Nasopharyngeal aspirate or swab
  • Throat swab
  • Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL)
  • Sputum

Diagnostic tests compared

TestWhat it detectsNotes
NAAT / PCRViral nucleic acid (RNA/DNA)Preferred method. Rapid and sensitive. Often done as a multiplex panel (e.g. influenza + RSV on one swab). Multiple gene targets for SARS-CoV-2: E, N, S, ORF1ab, RdRp.
Antigen test (lateral flow)Viral proteinRapid, home use. Less sensitive and specific than PCR. Used widely for COVID-19.
SerologyHost antibodies to virusNot routine. Delayed antibody response limits utility in acute illness.
Cell cultureLive virus growthSLOW — not routinely used for clinical diagnosis.

Emerging respiratory viral diseases

1. Avian influenza (bird flu)

  • Influenza A infection of birds — affects a wide range of avian species
  • At least 15 avian influenza subtypes known
  • Generally replicates inefficiently in humans; does not transmit well human-to-human
  • Large avian reservoir enables ongoing circulation (key concern: H5N1)
  • Rapid genetic mutation and reassortment can occur → pandemic risk
  • How humans get infected: direct contact with infected birds, contaminated surfaces (fomites), or airborne droplets/dust from birds
Ireland 2025: 5 turkey farm outbreaks of HPAI H5N1 (Nov–Dec 2025). Restrictions lifted 4 Jan 2026 following successful control. 68 wild birds tested positive. Europe 2025: ~700 poultry outbreaks.

2. MERS-CoV (Middle East Respiratory Syndrome)

  • Coronavirus, first identified 2012 (UAE)
  • 2628 laboratory-confirmed cases globally; majority from Saudi Arabia (2218 cases, 866 deaths)
  • Case fatality rate: 39%
  • Males affected more than females
  • Does not spread easily person-to-person unless close contact (e.g. unprotected care of infected patient)
  • Dromedary camels = main animal reservoir and likely source of human infection (exact route unknown)
  • ¾ of cases have underlying disease
  • Median incubation period: 5 days (range 2–15 days)
  • Transmission settings: sporadic community cases, family clusters, nosocomial acquisition, imported cases
39% case fatality rate — one of the most lethal known respiratory viruses

3. SARS-CoV-2 — see dedicated tab

The most recent and most impactful emerging respiratory virus, causing the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020.

SARS-CoV-2 / COVID-19

Scale and timeline

  • Novel coronavirus identified: 7 January 2020
  • Background: increase in pneumonia of unknown cause in Wuhan, China, December 2019
  • WHO declared global pandemic: 11 March 2020
  • Worldwide: >779 million cases, >7 million deaths
  • Ireland: first confirmed case 29 March 2020; >1.8 million cases; >9800 deaths

Virus structure

  • Positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus, enveloped
  • 4 main structural proteins:
  • S — spike Binds ACE2 receptor; main vaccine target
  • E — envelope
  • N — nucleocapsid
  • M — membrane
  • Also 16 non-structural proteins + 5–8 accessory proteins

Transmission

  • Primarily respiratory droplets
  • Also aerosols and contaminated surfaces → mucous membranes
  • Incubation: 1–14 days, median 5–6 days
  • Can transmit 1–3 days before symptom onset
  • Mild/moderate disease: shed virus up to 10 days
  • Severe disease: shed virus up to 20 days
  • Asymptomatic persons can transmit but for shorter periods

Clinical features

Common symptomsOther symptoms
Fever, cough, shortness of breath, ageusia (loss of taste), anosmia (loss of smell)Myalgia, headache, sore throat, nasal congestion, vomiting, diarrhoea, fatigue, brain fog
80% asymptomatic or mild · 15% moderate · 5% severe

Confirmed cases: self-isolate 5 full days from symptom onset (last 2 days fever-free); take precautions for the following 5 days.

Variants

  • SARS-CoV-2 evolves over time like all viruses
  • Most mutations have no impact on viral function
  • Variants of concern = those with evidence for enhanced transmission or clinical implications (e.g. Omicron BA subvariants)

Diagnosis

  • RT-PCR Detects viral RNA; rapid, sensitive; targets: E gene, N gene, S gene, ORF1ab, RdRp
  • Antigen test Detects viral protein; rapid, home use; less sensitive/specific
  • Sample: nasopharyngeal or throat swab

Treatments

  • Remdesivir — antiviral
  • Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (Paxlovid) — antiviral
  • Dexamethasone — immune modulator; use if patient requires oxygen
  • Tocilizumab — immune modulator

Management by setting

SettingKey interventions
HomeRestricted movements, self-isolation, GP/hospital monitoring as required
HospitalDroplet/airborne isolation, stratify severity, supplementary O2, VTE prophylaxis (LMWH), dexamethasone if needs O2
ICUPositioning (high supported sitting, prone position), airway clearance management, critical care

Coronavirus family context

4 endemic coronaviruses circulate in humans causing cold-like illness: OC43, 229E, NL63, HKU1.

Successive emergences: SARS-CoV (2003) → MERS-CoV (2012) → SARS-CoV-2 (2019). All likely originate from bat reservoirs, sometimes via intermediate animal hosts (civets for SARS-CoV, camels for MERS-CoV).

Huanan Seafood Wholesale Market in Wuhan identified as early epicentre of COVID-19 pandemic.

Prevention

  • Hand hygiene
  • Respiratory / cough etiquette
  • General hygiene measures (avoid touching face)
  • Vaccination

Practice MCQs

0 / 15 answered

1. A surgical intern presents in January with fever 39.3°C, myalgia, and lassitude. What does she most likely have?

Influenza. The abrupt onset, high fever, and prominent myalgia in the influenza season is classic influenza-like illness — NOT the common cold (which has no significant fever and symptoms mild and confined to the upper respiratory tract).

2. What is the difference between antigenic drift and antigenic shift? Which one causes pandemics?

Antigenic drift = minor changes from gradual accumulation of point mutations in HA/NA genes → yearly seasonal epidemics. Antigenic shift = major change from genetic reassortment when 2 different strains co-infect one cell → novel virus with new surface proteins → little or no population immunity → PANDEMIC. Shift causes pandemics.

3. What are the two key surface glycoproteins of influenza virus, and what does each do?

Haemagglutinin (HA) — binds to receptors on host cells (attachment step). Neuraminidase (NA) — cleaves sialic acid to release new virus particles from the cell and prevents clumping. NA is the drug target for oseltamivir and zanamivir (neuraminidase inhibitors).

4. What is the most common cause of severe lower respiratory tract infection in children under 2?

Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV). It is also the commonest cause of hospital admissions in young children. The classic presentation is bronchiolitis — acute inflammation of the bronchioles.

5. What are the clinical signs of RSV bronchiolitis? What do you expect on chest X-ray?

Clinical: respiratory distress with tachypnoea, nasal flaring, wheeze; apnoea; irritability; cyanosis; prominent intercostal retraction (lower airway obstruction). CXR: flat diaphragms and hyperlucent lower lung fields — indicating hyperinflation.

6. Which virus classically causes croup, and what does croup sound like clinically?

Parainfluenza virus is the most common cause. Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis) presents as a harsh brassy cough, worse at night. It is the 2nd most common cause of childhood respiratory hospitalisation after RSV.

7. A patient returns from Saudi Arabia with severe pneumonia (incubation ~5 days). Which emerging virus should you suspect?

MERS-CoV (Middle East Respiratory Syndrome coronavirus). First identified 2012, index case in UAE. Dromedary camels = main animal reservoir. Median incubation 5 days (range 2–15). Case fatality ratio ~39%. Majority of cases from Saudi Arabia.

8. What are the 4 main structural proteins of SARS-CoV-2 and which one is the drug/vaccine target?

S (spike), E (envelope), N (nucleocapsid), M (membrane). The spike protein (S) binds the ACE2 receptor and is the main target of COVID-19 vaccines.

9. Why should healthcare workers get the influenza vaccine every year?

~30% of influenza infections are asymptomatic but still infectious. HCWs can unknowingly transmit influenza to vulnerable hospitalised patients, and influenza can kill a vulnerable patient. HCWs are usually healthy adults who mount excellent vaccine responses, protecting patients who cannot adequately protect themselves.

10. Compare RT-PCR and lateral flow antigen testing for SARS-CoV-2.

RT-PCR: detects viral RNA, relatively rapid and sensitive, multiple gene targets (E, N, S, ORF1ab, RdRp), laboratory-based. Antigen test (lateral flow): detects viral protein, rapid, can be done at home by anyone, less sensitive and less specific than PCR. Both use nasopharyngeal or throat swab.

11. What antivirals are used for influenza? What class are they, and how do they work?

Neuraminidase inhibitors: Oseltamivir (oral — also used as post-exposure prophylaxis in at-risk patients) and Zanamivir (inhaled). They work by inhibiting neuraminidase, the enzyme that cleaves sialic acid to release new viral particles from infected cells.

12. Name the two approved passive immunisation options for RSV in infants in the EU. How do they differ?

1) Nirsevimab — long-acting monoclonal antibody given as a single IM injection directly to the infant (national pilot programme underway in Ireland). 2) RSVpreF (Abrysvo, Pfizer) — maternal vaccine; provides infant protection via transplacental antibody transfer. Not yet part of the free national programme in Ireland.

13. What is the preferred diagnostic test for respiratory viruses in clinical practice, and why are serology and cell culture not used routinely?

NAAT/PCR is preferred — detects viral nucleic acid, rapid and sensitive. Often performed as a multiplex panel (e.g. influenza + RSV on one swab). Serology is not routine because the delayed antibody response limits utility in acute illness. Cell culture is too slow for clinical diagnosis.

14. What percentage of COVID-19 infections are asymptomatic or mild? What are the classic symptoms?

80% are asymptomatic or mild. 15% moderate. 5% severe. Classic symptoms: fever, cough, shortness of breath, ageusia (loss of taste), anosmia (loss of smell). Other: myalgia, headache, sore throat, fatigue, GI symptoms.

15. Why do influenza patients get secondary bacterial pneumonia? Which bacteria are responsible?

Influenza causes loss of cilia in the respiratory epithelium → impaired mucociliary clearance → bacteria more easily colonise the lower respiratory tract. Main pathogens: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae (the bacterium — not the virus!), and Staphylococcus aureus.
