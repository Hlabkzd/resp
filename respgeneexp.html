Regulation of Gene Expression in Prokaryotes

Dr Lucienne Vassallo Gatt

Gene Expression — The Basics

Gene expression is the process by which genetic information is converted into a biologically functional molecule — either a protein or a functional RNA (the gene product).

Two classes of genes:
  • Constitutive genes — expressed continuously to maintain basic cellular functions.
  • Regulated genes — switched ON or OFF in response to environmental/metabolic demands. Critical for differentiation, development, and adaptation.

Gene Components

Every gene contains:

  • Exons — coding sequences that appear in mature mRNA
  • Introns — non-coding intervening sequences (spliced out in eukaryotes)
  • Start & Stop signals — mark where transcription/translation begins and ends
  • Regulatory control elements — DNA sequences that modulate transcription

Control of Transcription

Cis-acting elements — DNA sequences flanking the gene on the same chromosome (e.g. promoters, operators, enhancers). They have no product; they are sites on DNA.
Trans-acting factors — Molecules (usually proteins) that bind to cis-acting DNA sequences to activate or repress transcription. They are typically encoded on a different chromosome and diffuse to their target. Trans factors can be both activators and repressors.

In prokaryotes, trans-acting proteins bind regulatory elements in bacterial DNA to start or stop transcription.

The Operon Model

An operon is an entire segment of bacterial DNA comprising a cluster of genes arranged in sequence, transcribed as a single unit into one polycistronic mRNA. This allows co-regulation of functionally related genes with a single switch.

Two Major Examples

  • lac operon — inducible; normally OFF, turned ON when lactose is present (negative control + positive control)
  • trp operon — repressible; normally ON, turned OFF when tryptophan is abundant (negative control via co-repressor)

The lac Operon — Inducible System

Controls genes encoding enzymes for lactose metabolism in E. coli. It has dual control: negative control (repressor) and positive control (CAP/cAMP).

Structural Genes

GeneProtein EncodedFunction
lacZβ-galactosidaseCleaves lactose → glucose + galactose
lacYGalactoside permeaseMembrane transporter; brings lactose into the cell
lacAThiogalactosidase transacetylaseAcetylates non-metabolisable galactosides for excretion

Scenario A — Glucose present, No lactose → Operon OFF

  • Repressor protein (lacI product) binds the operator → blocks RNA polymerase → no mRNA, no enzymes
  • Glucose activates adenylyl cyclase inhibition → low cAMP → CAP unbound → CAP binding site empty → RNA pol cannot initiate efficiently (catabolite repression)
  • Double off: repressor blocks, and CAP positive signal absent

Scenario B — No glucose, Lactose present → Operon ON

  • A small amount of lactose enters via baseline permease → converted to allolactose (the true inducer)
  • Allolactose binds repressor → conformational change → repressor can no longer bind operator → operator free
  • Absence of glucose → adenylyl cyclase active → high cAMP → cAMP binds CAP → CAP-cAMP complex binds CAP binding site → stimulates RNA polymerase
  • Both barriers removed: transcription proceeds → lacZ, lacY, lacA expressed

Scenario C — Both glucose AND lactose present → Operon OFF

Critical exam point: Even though allolactose inactivates the repressor, glucose suppresses cAMP production → CAP binding site remains empty → RNA polymerase cannot efficiently initiate → operon is OFF.

This is catabolite repression — the cell preferentially uses glucose (the preferred energy source) over lactose.

Catabolite repression mechanism: Glucose inhibits adenylyl cyclase → ↓ cAMP → CAP not activated → even with repressor off, RNA polymerase lacks the positive boost needed. The CAP-cAMP complex is a classical example of a positive activator (trans-acting factor) binding a cis-acting element.

The trp Operon — Repressible System

Controls five genes (trpE, trpD, trpC, trpB, trpA) encoding enzymes for tryptophan biosynthesis. This is the opposite logic to the lac operon — the system is normally ON and turns OFF when tryptophan is available.

Repressor Control

  • The trp repressor protein alone is inactive (aporepressor) — cannot bind the operator on its own
  • Tryptophan acts as a co-repressor — it binds the aporepressor, causing a conformational change
  • Tryptophan-repressor complex → binds operator → blocks RNA polymerase → synthesis of trp biosynthesis enzymes stops
  • When tryptophan is absent → repressor dissociates from operator → RNA polymerase proceeds → tryptophan produced

Attenuation — Additional Fine-Tuning

The trp operon has a second, more nuanced control layer called attenuation, unique to bacteria (relies on coupled transcription-translation).

  • A leader sequence (attenuator region) precedes the structural genes
  • The leader mRNA can fold into two alternative secondary structures:
    1. A hairpin loop = transcription terminator → premature termination
    2. An antiterminator structure → allows read-through
  • The choice depends on ribosome position on the nascent leader mRNA
  • If tryptophan is abundant: charged tRNATrp available → ribosome translates smoothly through the leader → stalls at a specific position → terminator hairpin forms → transcription terminated prematurely
  • If tryptophan is scarce: uncharged tRNATrp → ribosome stalls earlier → antiterminator hairpin forms instead → transcription continues into structural genes
Why attenuation works only in prokaryotes: It requires the ribosome to be translating the mRNA as it is being transcribed — only possible because transcription and translation are coupled in the cytoplasm. Eukaryotes cannot do this (nucleus separates the two processes).

Regulation at the rRNA Level & Translation

Gene expression in prokaryotes can also be regulated after transcription, at the level of translation and ribosome function.

The Stringent Response (Amino Acid Starvation)

Trigger: Amino acid starvation → uncharged (empty) tRNAs accumulate in the A site of the 70S ribosome.

Uncharged tRNA in ribosomal A site
↓ activates

↓ catalyses: ATP + GDP →

↓ causes
Selective inhibition of transcription of rRNA, tRNA, and some mRNAs
Key nuance: ppGpp does NOT inhibit mRNAs coding for amino acid biosynthesis enzymes — so new amino acids can still be produced to relieve starvation. Only ribosome-related transcription is suppressed.

Stringent Controls

  • Minimise protein synthesis globally until amino acids become available again
  • Prevent wasteful production of ribosomes that have no substrates
  • This is an emergency energy-conservation response

Excess Ribosomal Proteins

Ribosomal proteins are made in equimolar amounts with rRNA. If rRNA synthesis falls (e.g. during starvation), excess ribosomal proteins accumulate. These can then bind to their own mRNA and inhibit translation as a feedback mechanism — preventing further ribosomal protein production.

Summary of Regulatory Levels in Prokaryotes

  • Primary: Transcriptional control (operons, repressors, activators)
  • Secondary: Attenuation (coupled transcription-translation hairpin)
  • Tertiary: Translational control (ppGpp stringent response, ribosomal protein feedback)

More Complex Gene Regulation in Eukaryotes

Eukaryotic gene regulation is substantially more complex, with control exerted at multiple stages. Most control still occurs at the transcriptional level, but additional layers exist.

Transcriptional Initiation Complex

RNA Polymerase II (the enzyme for mRNA genes in eukaryotes) cannot initiate transcription alone. It requires assembly of a full complex:

  • GTFs (General Transcription Factors) — assemble at the TATA box to form the preinitiation complex with Pol II
  • Activators (Specific Transcription Factors) — DNA-binding proteins that bind enhancer elements; have a DNA-binding domain and a transcription activation domain
  • Co-activators / Mediator — bridge between activators and the Pol II basal machinery

Regulation is achieved through specific combinations of these protein factors — combinatorial control.
