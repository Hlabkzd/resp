Regulation of gene expression in eukaryotes

Dr Lucienne Vassallo Gatt — RCSI Semester 2

The big picture

Eukaryotes control which proteins are made — and when — at multiple checkpoints. This gives extraordinary flexibility: every cell in your body has the same DNA, yet a neuron and a hepatocyte are completely different.

1. Chromatin structure
2. Transcription initiation
3. mRNA modification
4. Translation initiation
5. Protein modification/degradation

Memory hook — "Can Transcription Make The Protein?"
Chromatin → Transcription → mRNA modification → Translation → Protein degradation

Chromatin structure basics

In eukaryotes, DNA is wrapped around histone proteins (H2A, H2B, H3, H4) forming a nucleosome — the basic unit of chromatin. Histone H1 links nucleosomes together.

Whether a gene can be transcribed depends entirely on how tightly the DNA is packed.

Euchromatin (open)

Loosely packed histones. Transcription enzymes can access DNA. Genes are actively transcribed. Histone acetyltransferases (HATs) add acetyl groups → loosen packing.

Heterochromatin (closed)

Tightly packed histones. RNA polymerase cannot access DNA. Genes are silenced. Histone deacetylases (HDACs) remove acetyl groups → restore positive charge → tight coiling.

The acetylation mechanism
1
Histone tails carry positively charged lysine residues that bind tightly to the negatively charged DNA backbone.
2
HATs add acetyl groups (from Acetyl-CoA) to lysine. This neutralises the positive charge → DNA–histone interaction weakens → chromatin opens.
3
HDACs remove acetyl groups → positive charge restored → strong DNA–histone binding → chromatin condenses → gene silenced.

HATs = open chromatin = transcription ON. HDACs = closed chromatin = transcription OFF.

Transcription factors

Transcription factors (TFs) are proteins that help RNA polymerase II bind to the promoter region. Without them, RNA pol II cannot initiate transcription effectively.

TFs present

RNA polymerase binds the promoter → transcription begins → gene expressed

TFs absent

RNA polymerase cannot bind (or binds rarely) → gene not expressed

Enhancers and activators

Enhancers are DNA segments upstream of the promoter. Activator proteins bind enhancers → the enhancer–activator complex loops to interact with coactivators and TFs at the promoter → RNA pol II binding is facilitated.

Repressors bind to silencer sequences and block transcription machinery.

Key components at the promoter: TATA box, TATA-binding protein, basal transcription factors, coactivators, RNA polymerase II.

mRNA processing (pre-mRNA → mature mRNA)

After transcription in the nucleus, the pre-mRNA is modified before export to the cytoplasm. Three key modifications:

A
Removal of introns (splicing): Non-coding intron sequences are excised; exons are joined. Performed by the spliceosome.
B
5' capping: A 7-methylguanosine cap is added to the 5' end. Protects mRNA from degradation and assists ribosome binding.
C
3' polyadenylation: A poly-A tail (~200 adenine residues) is added to the 3' end. Stabilises the mRNA and aids nuclear export.
Alternative splicing

Different exons can be included or excluded from the mature mRNA, generating multiple different proteins from a single gene. This massively expands the proteome.

Example: a gene with exons 1–2–3–4–5 can produce Protein A (exons 1–2–3–4–5) or Protein B (exons 1–2–4–5) — exon 3 excluded.

RNA editing — ApoB example

ApoB mRNA is produced from the same gene in both liver and intestine, but in the intestine only, cytidine deaminase converts a C → U in codon CAA (glutamine), changing it to UAA — a stop codon.

Liver

No editing → full-length ApoB-100 (LDL transport)

Intestine

C→U editing → stop codon → truncated ApoB-48 (chylomicron assembly)

Same gene, same initial transcript → two completely different functional proteins via RNA editing.

Regulation of translation initiation

Regulatory (masking) proteins can bind to the 5' untranslated region (5' UTR) of mRNA and physically block ribosome attachment → translation cannot begin.

The poly-A tail requirement

mRNAs that lack a poly-A tail on the 3' end cannot be translated. This is exploited in development:

1
Unfertilised eggs store mRNAs without poly-A tails → translation is silenced
2
After fertilisation, a poly-A tail is added to these masked mRNAs
3
Ribosomes can now bind → rapid burst of protein synthesis to begin development

This allows fast developmental responses without waiting for new transcription.

Protein regulation by degradation

Cells control protein levels not just by making them, but by targeted degradation. Two main pathways:

Pathway 1 — Ubiquitin–proteasome system
1
Small globular protein ubiquitin is covalently attached to the target protein
2
Multiple ubiquitin molecules are added → forms a polyubiquitin chain (signal tag)
3
The polyubiquitinated protein is recognised by large barrel-shaped proteasomes
4
Proteasome unfolds and cleaves the protein into amino acids (ATP-dependent)
5
Ubiquitin molecules are recycled and amino acids re-enter the metabolic pool
Pathway 2 — Lysosomal degradation

Proteins are degraded inside lysosomes by acid hydrolases. This process does not require energy (ATP) — in contrast to the proteasome pathway. Typically handles extracellular/membrane proteins brought in by endocytosis.

Ubiquitin–proteasome

Cytoplasmic proteins, specific targeting via ubiquitin tag, ATP-dependent, ubiquitin recycled

Lysosomal acid hydrolysis

Membrane/extracellular proteins, non-specific, does NOT require energy

Hormones and gene programmes

Hormones switch gene programmes on or off by generating Hormone Response Elements (HREs) — cis-acting DNA sequences that bind trans-acting protein factors, ultimately controlling transcription.

Lipid-soluble hormones (steroids, e.g. cortisol)

Cross the cell membrane → bind intracellular receptors → receptor undergoes conformational change → exposes zinc-finger DNA-binding domain → receptor dimerises → complex enters nucleus → binds GRE (Glucocorticoid Response Element) → recruits coactivators + RNA pol II → gene transcription.

Water-soluble hormones (peptides, e.g. glucagon)

Cannot cross membrane → bind cell-surface receptors → activates adenylyl cyclase → ATP → cAMP → activates PKA → phosphorylates CREB transcription factor → phospho-CREB binds CRE (cAMP Response Element) in nucleus → activates RNA pol II → gene transcription.

Memory hook: Lipid-soluble hormones can "walk through walls" (membrane) and act directly. Water-soluble hormones can't enter, so they use a messenger (cAMP) — hence "second messenger" signalling.

Transferrin receptor — iron sensing

Transferrin is a plasma iron-transport protein. Its receptor (TfR) on cells is regulated post-transcriptionally by iron levels:

1
TfR mRNA has Iron-Responsive Elements (IREs) in its 3' UTR
2
IREs can bind Iron Regulatory Proteins (IRPs)
3
Low iron: IRPs bind IREs → mRNA stabilised → TfR synthesised → more iron taken up ✓
4
High iron: IRPs bind iron instead of IREs → IREs unbound → mRNA degraded → less TfR made → iron uptake decreases ✓

This is a negative-feedback loop: high iron → less TfR → less iron uptake. Elegant post-transcriptional regulation.

Summary comparison: ApoB vs Transferrin receptor

ApoB (RNA editing)

C→U base change creates a stop codon in intestine only. Two proteins from one gene: ApoB-100 (liver) and ApoB-48 (intestine). Tissue-specific editing.

Transferrin receptor (mRNA stability)

IRP binding to 3' UTR IREs stabilises mRNA. Regulated by intracellular iron levels. Classic example of post-transcriptional control by mRNA stability.
