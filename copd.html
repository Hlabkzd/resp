Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease

Pathophysiology, microscopic features, symptoms, signs, and differential diagnosis of COPD.

RCSI · Year 1 Semester 2 · Departments of Medicine & Pathology
What is COPD?
GOLD Definition: COPD is a heterogeneous lung condition characterised by chronic respiratory symptoms (dyspnoea, cough, sputum production, exacerbations) due to abnormalities of the airways (bronchitis, bronchiolitis) and/or alveoli (emphysema) that cause persistent, often progressive, airflow obstruction.
Spirometric Criterion

Airflow limitation defined as a post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC ratio < 0.70 (70%) that is not fully reversible.

  • FEV₁ — volume of air forcefully exhaled in the first second
  • FVC — total volume exhaled during a maximal expiratory effort
The Two Components
🫁 Chronic Bronchitis
Clinical definition: Productive cough for ≥ 3 months in each of 2 successive years, with other causes excluded.
  • Disease of mucus blocking airways
  • Common in cigarette smokers and urban dwellers
  • Airflow obstruction from peripheral bronchiole inflammation, fibrosis and co-existent emphysema
💨 Emphysema
Morphological definition: Permanent enlargement of airspaces distal to the terminal bronchioles with destruction of their walls, without obvious fibrosis.
  • Disease of loss of elastic tissue around acini and terminal bronchioles
  • Males > females; heavy smokers
  • Develops over many years; clinically significant in later life
Epidemiology
  • Affects >10% of the US population; ~200 million worldwide
  • 14% of male smokers vs 3% of non-smokers — smoking is not the only cause
  • 4th leading cause of death in the western world
  • The only common cause of death increasing in incidence
Aetiology
  • 90% of cases occur in chronic smokers; 25% of heavy smokers develop chronic bronchitis
  • Age (high risk), urban > rural environment
  • Air pollutants: sulphur dioxide, nitrogen dioxide
  • Dusty occupations: coal, quartz, silica, welding
  • Passive smoking
  • Low birth weight; chest infections in the first year of life (e.g. adenovirus)
Chronic Bronchitis — Pathogenesis
Core mechanism: Irritants → hypersecretion by bronchial mucus glands → gland hypertrophy + goblet cell metaplasia + inflammation → mucus plugging + airflow obstruction.
Sequence of Events
  1. Inhaled irritants trigger hypersecretion of mucus from large airways
  2. Irritants induce hypertrophy of bronchial mucus glands
  3. Metaplastic formation of mucin-secreting goblet cells in surface epithelium
  4. Inflammatory infiltrate: CD8+ T lymphocytes, macrophages, neutrophils
  5. Airways become swollen, erythematous, covered by mucopurulent secretions
Hallmarks (histology):
  • Mucus gland hypertrophy
  • Increased goblet cells
  • Loss of ciliated epithelial cells
  • Squamous metaplasia → dysplasia → squamous cell carcinoma (long-term risk)
Clinical Course
  • Cough with sputum may persist without significant ventilatory dysfunction early on
  • May progress to significant COPD with airflow obstruction → hypercapnia, hypoxaemia, cyanosis
  • Severe forms: pulmonary hypertension and cardiac failure (cor pulmonale)
  • Recurrent infections → respiratory failure
Emphysema — Anatomy
Respiratory Acinus

Portion of lung formed by branching of a single terminal bronchiole. Includes bronchiole, alveolar ducts, alveoli. The fundamental unit of gas exchange lost in emphysema.

Lung Lobule

Hexagonal area of parenchyma 1–2 cm across, outlined by fibrous septa. Contains 3–5 terminal bronchioles (= 3–5 acini). Grossly visible.

Emphysema — Pathogenesis
Protease–Antiprotease Imbalance: The key mechanism. Smoke/particulates activate macrophages & neutrophils → release of elastases & proteases → destruction of alveolar walls. Normally opposed by Alpha-1 Antitrypsin (A1AT).
Step-by-Step
  1. Airflow drops at terminal bronchioles → particulates deposit into adjacent alveoli
  2. Macrophages and neutrophils recruited; elastases turned on by particulates and smoke compounds
  3. Elastases and proteases degrade elastin in alveolar septa
  4. Imperfect repair → permanent enlargement of airspaces
Alpha-1 Antitrypsin (A1AT) Deficiency
  • Most common genetic cause of emphysema
  • Autosomal co-dominant disorder
  • Normal "M" allele → A1AT protein inhibits neutrophil elastase
  • "Z" mutation → severe protein deficiency → unopposed elastase → premature emphysema/COPD
  • Common in Ireland: 1 in 25 carry the "Z" mutation
  • Typically causes panacinar emphysema affecting lower lobes
Airflow Restriction — Mechanism
Key concept: Loss of elastic tissue in emphysema removes the structural support that holds small bronchioles open during expiration, leading to air trapping.
Bernoulli's Principle

In a constriction, fluid speed ↑ → pressure ↓. Applied to bronchioles: increased airflow speed through narrowed bronchioles → pressure drop → further constriction. Elastic tissue normally opposes this.

Poiseuille's Law

Resistance to flow is inversely proportional to radius⁴. Tiny reductions in bronchiolar radius cause enormous increases in resistance → even small narrowing → profound airflow limitation.

In COPD

During expiration, chest contracts → pressure gradient pushes air through small bronchioles (constriction point) → pressure drops → bronchioles tend to narrow further. Without elastic tissue to hold them open, they collapse → air trapping → increased residual volume → hyperinflation.

Cardinal Symptoms
SymptomDescriptionMechanism
Cough Initially intermittent → progressive Goblet cell hypertrophy, airway irritation, mucus stagnation, impaired mucociliary clearance
Sputum Mucoid/mucopurulent; ↑ volume & thickness during exacerbations Mucus hypersecretion; infections increase volume/purulence
Dyspnoea Exertional initially → rest in advanced disease Airway obstruction (oedema, mucus) + hyperinflation increase work of breathing
Wheeze / Chest tightness Intermittent → chronic & persistent Turbulent airflow through narrowed airways (expiratory sound); loss of elastic recoil
Wheeze vs Stridor: Wheeze is an expiratory sound (intrathoracic obstruction). Stridor is an inspiratory sound (extrathoracic/upper airway obstruction). Do not confuse them.
Cardinal Signs & Their Mechanisms
General Inspection
  • Pursed-lip breathing — creates back-pressure to stent open collapsed small airways
  • Prolonged expiratory phase — obstructed airflow takes longer to empty
  • Barrel chest (↑ AP diameter) — air trapping → hyperinflation expands chest anteroposteriorly
  • Tachypnoea — increased respiratory drive due to hypoxaemia/hypercapnia
  • Accessory muscle use — high work of breathing recruits scalenes, sternocleidomastoid
Face, Neck & Hands
  • Central cyanosis — hypoxaemia (SaO₂ < 85%)
  • Peripheral cyanosis — poor peripheral perfusion ± hypoxaemia
  • Tracheal tug — accessory muscle action pulling trachea down
  • Tar staining — marker of smoking history
  • Asterixis (flapping tremor) — CO₂ retention (hypercapnic encephalopathy)
  • Pulsus paradoxus — exaggerated ↓ in systolic BP during inspiration due to large intrathoracic pressure swings
Chest Examination
  • ↓ Chest expansion — already hyperexpanded; reduced inspiratory reserve volume
  • Hyper-resonant percussion — increased air in hyperinflated lungs
  • ↓ Breath sounds — reduced airflow through emphysematous lung
  • Wheeze — turbulent expiratory airflow in obstructed bronchioles
  • Prolonged expiratory time — airflow obstruction prolongs emptying
Cardiovascular — Cor Pulmonale
Cor Pulmonale: Right-sided heart failure caused by lung disease.

Chronic hypoxia → pulmonary arteriole vasoconstriction → pulmonary hypertension → ↑ right ventricular afterload → RV failure.

Signs: Elevated JVP, peripheral oedema, hepatomegaly, ascites.

Most real COPD patients lie on a spectrum between these two extremes rather than fitting neatly into one category.
COPD Exacerbation
Definition: In a patient with known COPD, an acute, sustained deterioration of ≥2 of: (1) ↑ sputum volume, (2) ↑ sputum purulence, (3) breathlessness.

Associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Very common cause of GP/hospital presentations. Management follows GOLD guidelines.
Differential Diagnosis of COPD
🫁 Respiratory
  • Asthma reversible
  • Bronchiectasis
  • Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency
  • Cystic fibrosis
  • Interstitial lung diseases
  • Tuberculosis
  • Pulmonary hypertension
🫀 Cardiac
  • Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)
  • Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF)

Both can cause exertional dyspnoea, wheeze ("cardiac asthma"), and bilateral basal crackles mimicking COPD.

COPD vs Asthma — Key Difference: Airflow obstruction in asthma is reversible (≥12% and 200mL improvement in FEV₁ post-bronchodilator). COPD obstruction is not fully reversible (FEV₁/FVC <0.70 persists).
Differential Diagnosis of Acute COPD Exacerbation
🫀 Cardiac
  • Acute decompensated heart failure
  • Acute myocardial infarction
  • Tachyarrhythmia
🫁 Pulmonary
  • Acute pulmonary embolus
  • Pneumonia
  • Asthma exacerbation
  • Pneumothorax
  • Mucus plugging
  • Inhaled foreign body

Questions answered correctly
Question 1
According to GOLD, COPD is defined spirometrically as a post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC ratio of less than:
A post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.70 (70%) confirms airflow obstruction in COPD. This ratio is the diagnostic spirometric criterion used globally.
Question 2
Chronic bronchitis is clinically defined as a productive cough for at least 3 months in each of how many successive years?
By clinical definition, chronic bronchitis requires a productive cough for ≥3 months in each of 2 successive years, with other causes of chronic sputum excluded.
Question 3
Which histological feature is NOT a hallmark of chronic bronchitis?
Alveolar wall destruction is the hallmark of emphysema, not chronic bronchitis. The hallmarks of chronic bronchitis are mucus gland hypertrophy, increased goblet cells, loss of ciliated cells, and possible squamous metaplasia.
Question 4
What type of emphysema is most strongly associated with spontaneous pneumothorax?
Distal acinar emphysema predominantly affects the distal part of the acinus, is most striking adjacent to the pleura, and is most often associated with spontaneous pneumothorax. Bullous emphysema can also rupture to cause pneumothorax but is a separate entity.
Question 5
Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency most characteristically causes which type of emphysema?
A1AT deficiency causes panacinar emphysema with lower lobe predominance. By contrast, smoking-related emphysema causes centriacinar emphysema that is worse in the upper lobes.
Question 6
In the pathogenesis of emphysema, which enzyme's activity is normally counteracted by Alpha-1 antitrypsin?
A1AT (Alpha-1 antitrypsin) is a potent inhibitor of neutrophil elastase. When A1AT is deficient, neutrophil elastase goes unopposed, degrading elastin in alveolar septa and causing premature emphysema.
Question 7
Bernoulli's principle contributes to air trapping in COPD because:
Bernoulli's principle states that where fluid speed increases (at a constriction), pressure decreases. In bronchioles during expiration, this pressure drop tends to further narrow the airway. Normally, elastic tissue holds the bronchioles open. In COPD, lost elastic tissue means the bronchioles collapse, trapping air.
Question 8
A 52-year-old man with COPD has a barrel-shaped chest. What is the primary mechanism?
In COPD, air is trapped in the lungs (increased residual volume) because obstructed, non-elastic airways cannot be emptied. This hyperinflation expands the chest in an anteroposterior direction, producing the characteristic barrel chest appearance.
Question 9
Which percussion finding is expected over a hyperinflated COPD chest?
Hyperinflated lungs contain increased air, producing a hyper-resonant percussion note. Dull or stony-dull notes suggest consolidation or effusion. Tympanic notes are heard over gas-filled hollow viscera (e.g. stomach).
Question 10
Cor pulmonale in COPD refers to:
Cor pulmonale (Latin: "pulmonary heart") is right-sided heart failure caused by lung disease. In COPD, chronic hypoxia triggers pulmonary arteriole vasoconstriction → pulmonary hypertension → increased right ventricular afterload → right ventricular failure. Signs include elevated JVP, peripheral oedema, hepatomegaly, and ascites.
Question 11
A patient with COPD has asterixis (flapping tremor of the hands). What does this indicate?
Asterixis in COPD is a sign of CO₂ retention (hypercapnia). Elevated CO₂ causes a metabolic encephalopathy that produces the characteristic flapping tremor. It is a marker of severe respiratory failure requiring urgent management.
Question 12
Comparing the "blue bloater" and "pink puffer" phenotypes, which feature best describes the blue bloater (chronic bronchitis dominant)?
The blue bloater phenotype (chronic bronchitis dominant) is characterised by earlier onset (40–45), abundant sputum, frequent infections, and cor pulmonale. The pink puffer (emphysema dominant) has severe early dyspnoea, scant sputum, rare infections, and hyperinflation with a small heart on CXR.
Question 13
An acute exacerbation of COPD is defined as a sustained deterioration of at least how many of the cardinal symptoms (↑ sputum volume, ↑ sputum purulence, breathlessness)?
A COPD exacerbation requires an acute, sustained deterioration of at least 2 of: increased sputum volume, increased sputum purulence, and breathlessness. It is a common cause of GP visits and hospital admissions, with significant morbidity and mortality.
Question 14
The Z mutation in the Alpha-1 antitrypsin gene has what approximate carrier frequency in Ireland?
Approximately 1 in 25 people in Ireland carry the "Z" mutation on one of their alleles. This mutation causes severe deficiency of the A1AT protein. Ireland has one of the highest prevalences of the Z allele in the world.
Question 15
Which of the following is NOT typically in the differential diagnosis of an acute COPD exacerbation?
An acute exacerbation differential always considers acute PE, pneumothorax, heart failure, pneumonia, mucus plugging, tachyarrhythmia, and MI. Asthma exacerbation (in known asthmatics) is also on the list — but "asthma in a patient with no prior respiratory history" would be a new diagnosis, not typically framed as a COPD exacerbation differential. The other three are core differential diagnoses taught in this lecture.
