Introduction & Epidemiology

Tuberculosis is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, an aerobic, intracellular gram-positive bacillus. It remains one of the leading infectious causes of death globally.

  • ~⅓ of the world's population infected — majority in Africa and Asia
  • Responsible for 1.6 million deaths worldwide in 2017 (20% HIV co-infected)
  • Growing incidence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains
  • Airborne transmission via respiratory droplets (coughing, sneezing, spitting)
  • Only a small inoculum is required to establish infection
Key stat: 90% of those infected develop latent TB (LTBI) — asymptomatic, non-infectious. Only 10% lifetime risk of progression to active disease (higher with HIV).
Risk Factors — Developed World
  • Contact with high-risk groups (e.g. healthcare workers, prisons, homeless shelters)
  • Origin from or frequent travel to high-incidence countries
  • Immune deficiency — HIV, corticosteroids, chemotherapy, anti-TNF agents, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, malnutrition
  • Lifestyle factors — drug/alcohol use, homelessness, overcrowding
  • Genetic susceptibility — polymorphisms affecting macrophage function
The Organism Path
Mycobacterium tuberculosis
  • Aerobic, obligate intracellular pathogen
  • Slow-growing (divides every ~18–24 hours)
  • Thick, waxy mycolic acid capsule — protects from reactive oxygen species in phagolysosome
  • Acid-fast — retains carbol-fuchsin dye on Ziehl–Neelsen stain (appears red/pink)
Beginnings of Infection Path
1
Inhalation
Mycobacteria reach the alveolar air sacs. Alveolar macrophages are the first host cell.
2
Phagocytosis attempt
Macrophage engulfs the bacterium into a phagosome, which fuses with the lysosome to form a phagolysosome. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) attempt to destroy the pathogen.
3
Mycolic acid protection
The waxy mycolic acid cell wall shields the bacterium from ROS. MTB survives and replicates intracellularly, eventually killing the macrophage.
4
Immune recruitment
Infected macrophages release chemokines → recruit neutrophils & more macrophages. TNF-α and IFN-γ accelerate inflammatory cell recruitment. T cells are critical for macrophage activation.
Granulomatous Inflammation Path

TB is the classic granulomatous inflammatory disease. Granulomas are aggregates of epithelioid macrophages.

  • Macrophages fuse to form Langhans giant cells (multinucleated)
  • With activated T cells: macrophages at centre, surrounded by a rim of lymphocytes
  • The bacillus uses the granuloma to evade the immune system
  • Bacilli inside the granuloma become dormant → latent infection
  • Centre of granuloma often develops caseous necrosis (cheese-like, white material)
Key immune players: CD4+ T cells activate macrophages to kill bacilli. CD8+ cytotoxic T cells release perforin/granzymes to destroy infected cells. Cross-talk via TNF-α, IFN-γ, and GM-CSF. If balance tips in favour of the bacterium → dissemination.
Primary Tuberculosis Path

Usually occurs in childhood. Following inhalation, a small subpleural focus of granulomatous inflammation develops in the mid-zone — the Ghon focus.

  • Lymphatic spread carries macrophages to tracheobronchial and mediastinal lymph nodes
  • Primary complex = Ghon focus + hilar/mediastinal lymph nodes
  • <5% develop active disease on initial contact; rises to ~10% by the first year
Natural History of Primary Complex
  • Healing (commonest) — Ghon focus undergoes complete fibrosis/calcification
  • Pleural effusion or tuberculous empyaema
  • Enlarged hilar nodes → bronchial obstruction
  • Damage to bronchial lymphatics → ulceration → bronchiectasis
  • TB bronchopneumonia (early)
  • Blood vessel invasion → haematogenous spread → miliary TB
Secondary (Reactivation) Tuberculosis Path

The majority of active TB cases result from reactivation of latent infection, sometimes years or decades after initial exposure.

  • Lesions occur in posterior subapical / upper lobe regions — aeration exceeds blood flow here, favouring growth of this aerobic organism
  • Caseous necrosis occurs; cell-mediated immunity kills many bacteria and macrophages
  • May heal with dense scar or a fibrosed caseous mass (tuberculoma)
Natural History of Secondary TB
  • Necrotising process erodes bronchus → cavity formation
  • Diffuse pulmonary fibrosis, pleural fibrosis, shrunken lungs
  • Vascular obliteration (endarteritis) — but rupture can cause haemoptysis
  • TB cavity super-infected by Aspergillus → mycetoma
  • TB bronchopneumonia
  • Miliary TB
Miliary Tuberculosis Path

Haematogenous spread of a large burden of bacilli into the bloodstream.

  • Lungs and all organs can be seeded
  • Miliary tubercles ~1–2 mm on gross pathology
  • Usually generalised; death most commonly from TB meningitis
  • May initially be confined to the lungs
Reactivation Risk Factors Path
  • HIV co-infection highest risk
  • Immunosuppressant therapy (steroids, anti-TNF agents)
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Chronic end-stage renal disease
  • Malnutrition
  • Aging
  • Gastrectomy
  • Silica dust exposure (coal workers)
  • Laboratory workers in contact with infected material
Non-Tuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM) Path
  • M. avium-intracellulare complex (MAC) — usually in HIV patients; nodular interstitial infiltrates
  • M. kansasii — pulmonary lung disease
  • M. marinum — soft tissue / skin infection; fish tank granuloma
Latent vs Active TB Med

Latent TB (LTBI)

  • ~90% of infected people
  • No symptoms or signs
  • Not transmissible
  • Dormant bacteria "walled off" in granulomas
  • CXR and bloods normal
  • 5–10% lifetime reactivation risk

Active TB

  • ~5% on initial exposure; ~5% reactivation
  • Symptomatic, signs present
  • Transmissible (pulmonary)
  • CXR often abnormal
  • Microbiological diagnosis required
On exposure: ~5% clear bacteria entirely, ~5% develop active disease, ~90% develop latent TB.
Symptoms — Pulmonary TB Med

Pulmonary TB is a bacterial pneumonia with an insidious onset — symptoms typically persist for weeks to months (unlike acute bacterial pneumonia which resolves in days).

  • Drenching night sweats
  • Weight loss / cachexia
  • Fever
  • Productive cough
  • Haemoptysis (coughing blood) — vascular erosion by cavity
Symptoms — Extra-Pulmonary TB Med

Constitutional symptoms (night sweats, weight loss, fever) are common across all sites. Site-specific symptoms:

  • TB lymphadenitis: swollen cervical / supraclavicular lymph nodes
  • TB osteomyelitis / Pott's disease: chronic back pain (spinal involvement), caseous material tracking along psoas muscles
  • TB meningitis: drowsiness, confusion, headache, photophobia, neck stiffness — often more vague onset than bacterial meningitis
  • Extra-pulmonary TB is generally not transmissible (no coughing up bacilli)
Signs Med
  • Cachexia — generalised wasting
  • Digital clubbing — due to suppurative lung disease
  • Coarse crackles on auscultation over affected lung area
  • Palpable lymph nodes — cervical / supraclavicular in TB lymphadenitis
  • Tenderness over bone in osteomyelitis
  • Kernig's sign / low GCS / drowsiness in meningitis
Investigating Latent TB Med

Goal: prove prior exposure to TB (not current active infection). Two methods:

Tuberculin Skin Test (Mantoux)

  • Intradermal injection of tuberculin PPD
  • Read at 72 hours — skin induration
  • Requires 2 patient visits
  • Subjective interpretation
  • Cheaper
  • Positive after BCG vaccination (false positive)

QuantiFERON (IGRA)

  • Blood test — measures IFN-γ release by T cells exposed to TB antigens
  • Single visit, objective result
  • Not affected by BCG vaccine
  • More expensive
  • Electronic record persists long-term
Important: Neither Mantoux nor QuantiFERON can diagnose active TB. A positive result only confirms prior exposure. Never use these tests to diagnose active TB.
Investigating Active TB Med

The diagnosis of active TB is microbiological — proving the presence of M. tuberculosis.

TestMethodSpeedKey Limitation
Culture Gold Standard Grows on Löwenstein–Jensen or MGIT media Days – 8 weeks Slow; determines full drug sensitivity
Microscopy (ZN stain) Ziehl–Neelsen stain; acid-fast bacilli appear red Hours Cannot identify species or sensitivities; may miss low-burden samples
TB GeneXpert (PCR) Detects TB DNA; also tests for Rifampicin resistance ~2 hours Cannot determine full drug sensitivities; culture still required
GeneXpert advantages: Result in 2 hours, works on microscopy-negative samples, simultaneously tests for Rifampicin resistance (the backbone of standard treatment).
Samples by Site
  • Pulmonary TB: sputum (for microscopy, culture, GeneXpert)
  • TB lymphadenitis: lymph node biopsy
  • TB osteomyelitis: bone biopsy
  • TB meningitis: lumbar puncture (CSF — lymphocytes, ↑protein, ↓glucose)
Non-Specific Investigations
  • Chest X-ray: classical upper-lobe cavitation, consolidation, calcified Ghon focus; raises suspicion but does NOT diagnose TB
  • CT thorax: better delineates cavitation, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, miliary pattern
  • Routine bloods: non-specific; ESR/CRP elevated. Normal in latent TB.
Latent TB Treatment Med

Eliminates dormant bacteria and prevents future reactivation. A common clinical scenario: screening with QuantiFERON before starting immunosuppressive therapy (e.g. anti-TNF agents).

RegimenDurationNote
Rifampicin4 monthsSingle drug; preferred if shorter course needed
Isoniazid9 monthsRequires pyridoxine (B6) supplementation to prevent peripheral neuropathy
A single drug is sufficient in latent TB because the bacterial burden is very low → low risk of resistance developing.

Legal note: Latent TB treatment can be refused by the patient. Active pulmonary TB patients can be detained under public health legislation if they refuse treatment.
Active TB Treatment — Standard Regimen Med

Always combination therapy (typically 4 drugs) to prevent resistance developing. Uses the mnemonic RIPE:

DrugPhaseActionKey Side Effect
Rifampicin2 months intensive + 4 months continuationBactericidal — inhibits RNA polymeraseOrange urine/secretions, hepatotoxicity, enzyme inducer
Isoniazid (INH)All 6 monthsBactericidal — inhibits mycolic acid synthesisPeripheral neuropathy (give pyridoxine), hepatotoxicity
Pyrazinamide (PZA)First 2 monthsBactericidal in acidic environment (inside macrophages)Hepatotoxicity, hyperuricaemia, gout
EthambutolFirst 2 monthsBacteriostatic — inhibits arabinosyl transferase (cell wall)Optic neuritis (check visual acuity)
Standard regimen: 2 months RIPE → 4 months RI (total 6 months for drug-sensitive pulmonary TB). Duration extends to 2 years for drug-resistant TB or CNS disease.
Why 4 drugs? Resistance mutations occur spontaneously. With millions of bacteria in active disease, resistance to any single drug is almost guaranteed. Each additional drug provides coverage against resistant mutants, making combined resistance extremely unlikely.
Drug-Resistant TB Med
  • MDR-TB: resistant to at least Rifampicin and Isoniazid
  • XDR-TB: MDR-TB + resistance to fluoroquinolones and injectable second-line agents
  • Requires longer treatment with second-line agents (e.g. bedaquiline, linezolid, clofazimine)
  • Associated with HIV co-infection, incomplete courses, and poor adherence
  • GeneXpert detects Rifampicin resistance in hours — key for early identification

Pathological pattern at any site: tissue destruction, fibrosis & scarring, tuberculoma with mass effect, dense fibrinous exudate, cold abscesses.
CNS Tuberculosis Path
  • Spread via blood to subarachnoid space
  • Dense fibrous exudate at base of brain
  • Numerous white tubercles adjacent to blood vessels
  • CSF findings: lymphocytes, raised protein, low glucose
  • Can form TB cerebral abscess (tuberculoma)
  • Untreated TB meningitis is almost universally fatal
Bone TB (Pott's Disease) Path
  • Haematogenous spread to spine and growing ends of long bones
  • Vertebral destruction + caseous material tracks along psoas muscles → cold abscess
  • Pott's disease = TB spondylitis → kyphosis (gibbus deformity)
  • Also causes TB osteomyelitis of long bones and TB arthritis
Diagnosis of EPTB Path
  • AFB smear has low sensitivity on extra-pulmonary specimens
  • Tissue biopsy is often required — demonstrates caseating granulomas histologically
  • GeneXpert can be applied to biopsy material
  • Culture remains gold standard for diagnosis and sensitivities
  • Diagnosis often delayed due to slow, non-specific presentation
TB & HIV Co-Infection Med
  • TB incidence closely mirrors HIV incidence — highest in Africa, Indian subcontinent, Eastern Europe, Russia
  • HIV is present in ~10% of TB cases worldwide
  • TB is responsible for ~¼ of all AIDS-related deaths
  • HIV destroys CD4+ T cells → cannot activate macrophages → granulomas fail to contain MTB → dissemination
Why HIV dramatically increases TB risk: CD4+ T cells are essential for macrophage activation (the key killing mechanism for MTB). HIV depletes CD4+ cells, collapsing the granuloma's containment and causing latent TB to reactivate. The risk of reactivation increases from 10% lifetime to ~10% per year with untreated HIV.
Management of TB/HIV Co-Infection Med
  • Requires close collaboration between respiratory/ID physicians and HIV specialists
  • Treat TB first, then introduce antiretroviral therapy (ART) — timing depends on CD4 count
  • Drug interactions: Rifampicin is a potent CYP450 inducer → reduces levels of many antiretrovirals (particularly protease inhibitors and NNRTIs). Rifabutin may be substituted.
  • Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) can occur when ART is introduced — worsening TB paradoxical reaction as immunity recovers
  • MDR-TB is disproportionately associated with HIV co-infection
In HIV, TB presentation may be atypical: lower-lobe or diffuse disease on CXR (not classical upper-lobe cavitation), more extra-pulmonary disease, smear-negative pulmonary TB is more common, miliary TB more frequent.
